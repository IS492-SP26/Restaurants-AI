## Interviewer: Mubtasim Raad
## Interviewee: Shah, Owner of a Family Bengali Restaurant (Jamaica, NY)
## Experience: 17 Years in Business
## Location: Jamaica, Queens, New York
## Notes: Part of this interview was conducted in Bengali, the transcript below has been translated to english. 

### Q: What year did you start your restaurant business?
Shah: Well, let’s see... if we are sitting here in 2026 and I’ve been behind this counter for 17 years, that means I opened the doors back in 2009. Back then, starting a business right after the big financial crash of 2008 was a gamble. People thought I was crazy! But I knew our community needed a place that felt like home. My family and I put everything we had into this—every penny and every hour. Starting in 2009 taught me one thing very quickly: you have to be lean and you have to be tough.

### Q: When you were starting out, what were the initial struggles you faced for starting this business?
Shah: I didn't have any fancy tools back then—I just had my brothers, my wife, and a lot of caffeine. Looking back, I hit four "walls." First was the paperwork process. I spent months running between the Department of Health and the fire inspectors. One week we were ready, and the next, an inspector told us our sink was three inches too far from the prep table. Second was hidden costs. I didn't budget for a $5,000 grease trap repair or massive security deposits. Third was staffing. Finding 10 people who care about the quality of the Bhorta as much as I do was a nightmare. Finally, market noise. There were already established spots; I had to prove why people should come to the "new guy."

### Q: Did you have to deal with any food safety complications or ethics? If yes, how did you do it?
Shah: In 17 years, if someone tells you they’ve never had a complication, they are lying. Early on, we had a cooling issue. If you put a massive, piping-hot pot of goat curry straight into the fridge, the middle stays warm—that’s a bacteria playground. We almost failed an inspection. I had to invest in blast chillers and make sure everyone got their Food Protection Certificate.
On ethics, "Halal" is a sacred trust. Sometimes suppliers offered "regular" meat cheaper when they ran short, but I refused. If I sell one "fake" plate, I lose 17 years of reputation. We also handle food waste by partnering with local mosques so that nothing good goes to waste, but nothing "expired" hits a customer's plate.

### Q: When starting out how did you research the market and your competitors and what was the hardest part about it?
Shah: It was all "boots on the ground." I spent three weeks sitting at the bus stop on Hillside Avenue counting people. I’d watch who walked by with bags from other shops and what time they lined up. I also visited every competitor within 10 blocks. I’d eat there and look at their trash! If the trash was full of half-eaten rice, it meant their portions were too big or the food was bad. The hardest part was the "Ghost" data. You don't know what you don't see. For example, in a college town like yours, if you research in the summer, you won't see the student rush, and if you research in the winter, you won't see how empty the streets get when they leave.

### Q: How much time did it take you to do the market research, and what was the cost?
Shah: It was a second full-time job. It took me nearly 5 months of active research while I was still working my other job. The cost? Probably around $3,500 to $5,000 back in 2009. That includes $1,500 just for eating at other restaurants to "spy" on them, gas money, and paying an old consultant friend $1,000 to look at my building. He found a moisture problem in the basement that would have rotted my dry goods. That $1,000 saved me $20,000 later.

### Q: Were you able to open the business within the time you intended? If not, what caused the delay?
Shah: Hah! In this business, "on time" is a fairy tale. I intended to open in six months. It took eleven. Those five extra months of rent nearly bankrupted me. The delays came from permits—the city took eight weeks to review my ventilation plans just to reject them over a wooden beam. Then my contractor vanished for a bigger job in Manhattan, and the gas company took a month just to turn on the meters. You’re at the mercy of people who don't care that you're paying rent on an empty building.

### Q: I told you about the AI agent we are building... how would you want it to help you, if you were starting out today?
Shah: I don't know much about "agents," but if this tool can see around corners, then it's worth its weight in gold. I’d want it to be a "Navigator" that tells me exactly which permits I need and in what order so I don't waste time. I'd want it to be a "Student-Cycle Predictor"—tell me when the students disappear so I can cut my staff from 10 to 3 before I lose all my money. Most importantly, it needs to be a "Mean Uncle", a critic that tells me my plan is risky, my rent is too high, or that there are already too many Biryani spots nearby. If your tool is too "nice," it’s useless to a guy like me.

## Key Takeaways for Project Evaluation:
### 1. The "Pre-Revenue" Gap

Reality: Opening takes 1.5x longer than planned (e.g 6 months becomes 11).

Risk: You must budget for "dead rent"—paying for the space while waiting on permits and utility inspections without any income.

### 2. Regulatory Sequencing

Reality: One mistake in the order of permits (like kitchen ventilation) can halt the entire project for months.

Risk: Founders need a specific, step-by-step "order of operations" for local city departments.

### 3. Seasonal Market Volatility

Reality: In a college town like Champaign, the "Ghost Town" effect (summer/winter breaks) is the biggest threat to cash flow.

Risk: The plan must include a strategy for who the customer is when the students are gone.

### 4. Hidden "Physical" Costs

Reality: Infrastructure issues (grease traps, plumbing, basement moisture) are $10k–$20k "surprises" that aren't in generic templates.

Risk: A manual site inspection or specialized consultant is often more valuable than a digital business plan.

### 5. The "Mean" Feedback Loop

Reality: Nice advice is not helpful.

Risk: A tool is only useful if it aggressively identifies unrealistic labor costs or over-saturated menu concepts.
